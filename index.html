Job Market Paper

Data Technology and Market Efficiency

Abstract

How will advances in data technology affect market equilibrium and informational efficiency when adoption by investors is uneven? I show that data technology development and dissemination improve asset managers' stock-picking ability, especially for those with larger shares of data-science labor. Using U.S. institutional holdings and employment records, I estimate information production across investors and a demand system with heterogeneity in private information and learning from prices. I estimate investors' private information from holdings' forecasting power for future payoffs beyond public information and prices. Data technology developments are captured by increases in data scientist productivity. Estimates show that better-informed investors have more elastic demand, while learning from prices makes demand 61 percent less elastic at the median informative stock. A counterfactual advance calibrated to more than a decade of data scientist productivity growth raises price informativeness by 26 percent, more for small stocks. New technology adopters crowd out other investors, increasing market segmentation as stocks' investor bases diverge. Aggregate demand becomes more inelastic for 47 percent of stocks.

Understanding Rationality and Disagreement in House Price Expectations

With Stijn Van Nieuwerburgh and Wang Renxuan

Review of Financial Studies, 2026 (Editor's Choice)

Abstract

Professional house price forecast data are consistent with a rational model where agents must learn about the parameters of the house price growth process and the underlying state of the housing market. Slow learning about the long-run mean generates overreaction to forecast revisions and a modest response of forecasts to lagged realizations. Heterogeneity in signals and priors about the long-run mean helps the model account for cross-sectional dispersion in forecasts. Introducing behavioral frictions helps improve the model's predictions for short-horizon overreaction and dispersion. Using a cross-section of forecasters and a term structure of forecasts are crucial for inference.

Working Papers

The Limits of AI Trading

With Winston Wei Dou, Itay Goldstein, and Liyan Yang

Abstract

This paper develops a framework for AI-human interactions in financial markets, in which AI investors learn the price formation process through Q-learning while human investors form beliefs through level-k thinking, capturing how the two types differ in learning about the market environment. We show that for any human behavior, AI trading converges to a rational strategy that correctly understands human trading and the true price function. This convergence facilitates identifying two limits humans place on AI performance: AI's information advantage is bounded by human information, and human sophistication limits AI sophistication.

Conference presentations: NBER SI Financial Market Structure, WFA, EFA, AFA, CICF

Inelastic Demand at the Extensive and Intensive Margins

With Xindi He and Allen Hu

Abstract

We decompose investor demand into its extensive margin—initiating or liquidating a position—and intensive margin—scaling an existing position. Extensive demand is economically large, accounting for 40% of institutional and 80% of retail flows, and its estimated price elasticity is closer to zero and often positive. Extensive demand is primarily driven by past returns and attention, whereas intensive demand reflects fundamentals. To account for these findings, we develop a Grossman-Stiglitz-style model of inelastic markets in which extensive flows act as unobservable endogenous demand shocks and investors learn from prices based on their subjective expectations about these flows. Learning from prices leads to a more inelastic, and even upward-sloping, extensive demand curve. Embedding the model in an asset demand system, we structurally estimate both elasticities under alternative specifications of flow expectations and find that extrapolative expectations best fit the data. Reduced-form evidence and counterfactual analysis both show that as the share of extensive demand rises, prices, volatility, and firm-specific price informativeness increase, while market-wide informativeness declines.

Conference presentations: NBER SI Financial Market Structure, HEC-McGill Winter Finance Workshop
